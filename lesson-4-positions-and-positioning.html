Positions & Positioning

Your position is your name. Your positioning is your job. Knowing you're a winger is the easy part — knowing where a winger should be right now is the part that wins hockey games.

They're only one syllable apart, but position and positioning mean very different things — and mixing them up is the single most common reason a young player looks lost out there. Knowing you're a winger is the easy part. Knowing where a winger should be right now, as the puck moves, is the part that actually wins hockey games.

This Lesson's Focus Know your spot. Hold your spot.

Two Words That Sound the Same

🏷️
Position
Answers: What am I?

The role you're assigned before the puck drops: center, left wing, right wing, defense, or goalie. It's on the lineup card, and it doesn't change during your shift.

📍
Positioning
Answers: Where should I be right now?

Where you actually are on the ice at this exact second. It changes constantly — every time the puck moves, your positioning should move with it.

💡 Coach's Tip: You can have the right position and terrible positioning. A winger who follows the puck into the corner is still a winger — but he's not where his team needs him. Good positioning is what makes your position mean something.

The Five Positions

Three forwards, two defensemen, one goalie. Here's what each one is responsible for.

C
Center

The busiest player on the ice. Takes faceoffs, supports the puck low in our own end, and covers the middle of the ice from end to end.

LW
Left Wing

Lives on the left side of the ice. Covers the left boards on breakouts and watches the opposing defenseman on that side in our zone.

RW
Right Wing

Same job, opposite side. Stays on the right, provides a target on the boards, and covers the right point in our defensive zone.

D
Defense

Two of them, left and right. Defend our net first, keep opponents to the outside, start the breakout, and hold the blue line on offense.

G
Goalie

The last line of defense. Stops pucks, controls rebounds, and talks to teammates — the goalie sees the whole ice better than anyone.

Going Deeper · Reading the Play

The Critical Points

  • 1Don't be a puck magnet. The single biggest mistake in youth hockey: everyone chasing the puck at once. If a teammate already has it covered, your job is to be somewhere else — open and useful.
  • 2Stay in your lane. The ice has three lanes — left, middle, right. Wingers hold the outside lanes, the center works the middle. Three players in one lane means two lanes are wide open for the other team.
  • 3Support the puck. Whoever has the puck needs options. Be close enough to receive a pass but far enough that one opponent can't cover you both — that's a passing triangle.
  • 4Stay on the defensive side. In our own zone, be between your opponent and our net. If they're closer to our goalie than you are, they get the easy chance.
  • 5Head on a swivel. Positioning starts with knowing what's around you. Look before you get the puck, not after — check your shoulders so you already know your options.
  • 6Protect the house. The area in front of our net is the most dangerous ice on the rink. Defend the middle first and force the other team to the outside.

Don't Chase the Puck

A puck magnet is a player who chases the puck everywhere. When everyone swarms the puck, the rest of the ice is left wide open — and good teams will find it.

Stay in Your Lane

Left, middle, and right. Left wing takes the left lane, right wing the right lane, and the center works the middle. Staying spread out stretches the other team.

Support the Puck

Give your teammate a passing option — close enough to reach, far enough apart that one defender can't cover you both. Think of forming a triangle with the puck carrier.
